Abstract

A full-length fictional exam to train for the Midterm (45 min, Modules 1–5) and the Final (90 min, Modules 1–10).

This page is an original 2-hour (120 min), 100-point mock exam covering all ten modules of the course. It is entirely fictional: the café “Le Relais du Port” and its numbers are invented for this exam, and the only real course data used is a printed 10-day extract of The Wandering Fork (Harbour, days 91–100) plus references to payroll-skew.csv and the Campus franchise. Solutions are collapsible and mapped back to the module that covers each question, so you can pinpoint what to revise.

1 Rules and format

  • Duration: 120 minutes. Total: 100 points.
  • Individual work. Calculator allowed. No Excel needed: every dataset you need is printed on this page or is one of the small course CSVs (payroll-skew.csv, wandering-fork.csv) described on the dataset page.
  • Conventions (same as the whole course — see the glossary):
Question Convention Excel
Quartiles and percentiles Position \(p\,(n+1)\) with linear interpolation QUARTILE.EXC, PERCENTILE.EXC
Variance and standard deviation Sample formulas (\(n-1\)) VAR.S, STDEV.S
Skewness Adjusted Fisher–Pearson coefficient SKEW
Classes Half-open \([a, b[\): lower bound included, upper bound excluded —

2 Part A — Multiple choice (10 × 2 = 20 points)

Choose the single best answer.

A1. In the Wandering Fork dataset, staff_on_duty (the number of employees working a given day) is: (a) qualitative nominal (b) qualitative ordinal (c) quantitative discrete (d) quantitative continuous

A2. In a frequency table of a qualitative variable with total \(n\), the relative frequency of modality \(i\) is: (a) \(n_i \times n\) (b) \(n_i / n\) (c) \(n - n_i\) (d) \(n_i / k\)

A3. Sturges’ rule gives \(k \approx 1 + 3.3\,\log_{10}(n)\) classes. For \(n = 240\) observations, \(k \approx 8.85\), so you would use about: (a) 5 classes (b) 7 classes (c) 9 classes (d) 24 classes

A4. In payroll-skew.csv the mean salary is €47,231 and the median is €38,050 (the CEO earns €180,000). To report a “typical” salary you should report: (a) the mean, because it uses every observation (b) the median, because the distribution is right-skewed with an outlier (c) the mode (d) either one, they are equal here

A5. With \(n = 10\) sorted values, the course convention places \(Q_1\) at rank \(0.25 \times (10+1) = 2.75\). \(Q_1\) is therefore: (a) the 2nd value (b) the 3rd value (c) between the 2nd and 3rd values, three-quarters of the way toward the 3rd (d) the average of the 2nd and 3rd values

A6. The sample variance (VAR.S) divides the sum of squared deviations by \(n-1\) rather than \(n\) because: (a) it makes the variance smaller (b) one degree of freedom is spent estimating the mean — the deviations \(x_i - \bar{x}\) always sum to 0 (c) Excel requires it (d) \(n-1\) is always the larger divisor

A7. Excel’s SKEW returns \(-0.50\) for a dataset. This indicates: (a) a right-skewed distribution (b) a left-skewed distribution (c) a perfectly symmetric distribution (d) a bimodal distribution

A8. To show the relationship between two quantitative variables (e.g. customer_count and daily_revenue_eur) you use: (a) a line chart (b) a pie chart (c) a scatter plot (d) a histogram

A9. A true property of the least-squares regression line \(\hat{y} = a + b\,x\) is: (a) it always passes through the origin (b) it always passes through the point of means \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) (c) its slope is always between \(-1\) and \(1\) (d) it minimizes the sum of absolute deviations

A10. Over the Campus franchise’s 120 days, \(r(\text{temperature\_c}, \text{daily\_revenue\_eur}) = -0.12\), while rainy days visibly earn less than sunny days. A manager concludes “temperature drives revenue”. The best criticism is: (a) \(r\) is always positive (b) \(r\) measures linear association only — here \(|r| \approx 0.12\) shows no linear link — and correlation is not causation; the relevant variable is weather, not temperature (c) \(r\) should be computed on 30 days only (d) nothing — the conclusion is correct

3 Part B — Le Relais du Port, a fictional harbour café (30 points)

“Le Relais du Port” is a small invented café. Here is its daily revenue (€) over 10 consecutive days in June:

\[180,\ 200,\ 220,\ 230,\ 240,\ 240,\ 260,\ 280,\ 300,\ 620\]

(The €620 day was a private booking — do not remove it.)

(a) (6 pts) Build the class frequency table with classes \([150,200[\), \([200,250[\), \([250,300[\), \([300,700[\). Give the absolute frequencies \(n_i\), the densities \(n_i/h_i\) (the class widths differ — check this), the cumulative frequencies \(N_i^{+}\), and the cumulative shares \(F_i^{+}\) in %. Which class is the modal class?

(b) (4 pts) From the cumulative curve: (i) the share of days with revenue below €250; (ii) an estimate of \(P_{75}\), the revenue below which 75% of days fall (interpolate linearly between class bounds).

(c) (6 pts) Compute the five-number summary of the raw data under the course convention (QUARTILE.EXC, rank \(p\,(n+1)\) with interpolation), the IQR, the fences, and the outliers. Sketch the boxplot.

(d) (6 pts) Compute the mean, median and mode of the raw data; then the grouped mean (class midpoints) and the grouped mode from the table in (a).

(e) (4 pts) Compute the sample variance \(s^2\), the sample standard deviation \(s\), and the coefficient of variation \(CV\).

(f) (4 pts) Compute the Fisher–Pearson skewness (SKEW) by hand. Hint: express the deviations in tens of euros so the cubes stay small. Interpret the sign.

4 Part C — The Wandering Fork: Harbour, days 91–100 (35 points)

The table below is an extract of wandering-fork.csv (Harbour franchise, days 91–100):

day date day_of_week weather promo_active customer_count daily_revenue_eur satisfaction_rating
91 2026-07-05 Sunday Rainy No 7 119.97 1
92 2026-07-06 Monday Sunny Yes 35 549.51 4
93 2026-07-07 Tuesday Sunny Yes 29 401.30 5
94 2026-07-08 Wednesday Cloudy No 11 137.45 4
95 2026-07-09 Thursday Cloudy No 24 271.02 2
96 2026-07-10 Friday Rainy No 21 267.04 3
97 2026-07-11 Saturday Cloudy No 22 329.19 3
98 2026-07-12 Sunday Sunny No 46 634.78 3

(a) (5 pts) Give the statistical type of weather, promo_active and satisfaction_rating. Build the frequency tables for weather and for satisfaction_rating (categories in order 1–5), give the modal category of each, and the median satisfaction rating.

(b) (6 pts) For daily_revenue_eur: five-number summary (course convention), IQR, fences, outliers. What does the boxplot look like?

(c) (4 pts) For daily_revenue_eur: mean, sample standard deviation, \(CV\). Compare the mean and the median — what does the gap suggest?

(d) (8 pts) Build the contingency table promo_active (rows) × revenue class (columns: \([0,200[\), \([200,400[\), \([400,600[\), \([600,900[\)). Show counts and row percentages (conditional distribution of revenue given promo status). Do promo days and non-promo days have different revenue profiles?

(ii) Compute by hand — show \(\sum x\), \(\sum y\), \(\sum x^2\), \(\sum y^2\), \(\sum xy\) — the covariance, \(r\), \(r^2\), the slope \(b\) and the intercept \(a\). (iii) Predict the revenue of a 40-customer day; is this prediction reliable? (iv) Verify that the line passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\).

5 Part D — Short answers (15 points)

D1. (5 pts) Open payroll-skew.csv (11 salaries, one CEO at €180,000). Compute the mean and the median of annual_salary_eur. Which of the two is the better “typical salary” here, and why (mention the outlier rule if you use it)?

D2. (5 pts) Over the full Campus franchise (120 days), customer_count vs daily_revenue_eur gives \(r = 0.970\), \(r^2 = 0.940\), slope \(b = 12.30\) € per customer, intercept \(a = -22.75\). (i) Interpret \(r\) in one sentence, then \(r^2\) in one sentence. (ii) Predict the revenue of a 120-customer day. Is this prediction reliable? Why or why not?

D3. (5 pts) Using your Part C(d) result, write at most three sentences for the truck owner answering: “Do promotions seem to boost revenue?” Include the appropriate caution.

6 Solutions

Work through the exam before opening these. Each solution ends with the module to revise if you missed the question.

6.1 Part A

CautionA1 (Module 1) — answer (c)

staff_on_duty is counted in whole people: its possible values are isolated points (0, 1, 2, …), so it is quantitative discrete. Nominal/ordinal are qualitative; continuous variables can take any value in an interval.

Revise: Module 1.

CautionA2 (Module 2) — answer (b)

\(f_i = n_i / n\): the count of the modality divided by the total. It always lies between 0 and 1 and the \(f_i\) sum to 1.

Revise: Module 2.

CautionA3 (Module 3) — answer (c)

\(k \approx 1 + 3.3 \times \log_{10}(240) = 1 + 3.3 \times 2.380 \approx 8.85\), rounded to about 9 classes.

Revise: Module 3.

CautionA4 (Module 4) — answer (b)

The CEO’s €180,000 is a \(1.5 \times IQR\) outlier (upper fence ≈ €72,878) and pulls the mean up: the distribution is right-skewed (SKEW ≈ +3.12), so the median is the honest “typical” salary.

Revise: Modules 4, 5, 7.

CautionA5 (Module 5) — answer (c)

Rank \(2.75\) means: take the 2nd value and add \(0.75 \times\) (3rd − 2nd). This is exactly what QUARTILE.EXC does.

Revise: Module 5.

CautionA6 (Module 6) — answer (b)

The \(n\) deviations \(x_i - \bar{x}\) sum to 0, so only \(n-1\) are free; dividing by \(n-1\) (Bessel’s correction) makes \(s^2\) an unbiased estimator of the population variance.

Revise: Module 6.

CautionA7 (Module 7) — answer (b)

Negative SKEW = left-skewed: the long tail drags toward low values, and typically \(\bar{x} < Me < Mo\). (Right-skewed is positive; 0 is symmetric.)

Revise: Module 7.

CautionA8 (Module 8) — answer (c)

A scatter plot (XY) shows the association between two quantitative variables. A line chart implies a sequence in \(X\); a histogram or pie chart is for one variable.

Revise: Module 8.

CautionA9 (Module 9) — answer (b)

\(a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x}\) by construction, so the line always passes through \((\bar{x}, \bar{y})\). The slope \(b = \text{Cov}(X,Y)/s_X^2\) can be any real number and the least-squares criterion minimizes squared deviations.

Revise: Module 9.

CautionA10 (Module 10) — answer (b)

\(r = -0.12\) says there is essentially no linear link between temperature and revenue (\(r^2 \approx 0.014\)). The weather category is what separates good from bad days — a contingency-table question (Module 8), not a correlation one. And even a large \(r\) would not prove causation.

Revise: Modules 8, 9, 10.

6.2 Part B

CautionB(a) — class table (Module 3)

Sorted data: 180, 200, 220, 230, 240, 240, 260, 280, 300, 620 (\(n = 10\)).

Class (€) \(h_i\) \(n_i\) density \(n_i/h_i\) \(N_i^{+}\) \(F_i^{+}\)
\([150,200[\) 50 1 0.020 1 10%
\([200,250[\) 50 5 0.100 6 60%
\([250,300[\) 50 2 0.040 8 80%
\([300,700[\) 400 2 0.005 10 100%

The last class is 8× wider, so its density (0.005), not its raw count, is what the histogram bar height must show. Modal class: \([200,250[\) with \(n = 5\).

Revise: Module 3.

CautionB(b) — reading the cumulative curve (Module 3)
  1. Below €250: \(F^{+}\) at the upper bound 250 is 60% (6 of 10 days).
  2. \(P_{75}\): 75% lies inside \([250,300[\) because \(F^{+}\) goes from 60% (at 250) to 80% (at 300). Linear interpolation: \(250 + \frac{75-60}{80-60}\times 50 = 250 + 37.5 = \mathbf{287.5}\) €.

Revise: Module 3.

CautionB(c) — five-number summary, fences, boxplot (Module 5)
  • min = 180
  • \(Q_1\): rank \(0.25 \times 11 = 2.75\) → between 200 (2nd) and 220 (3rd): \(200 + 0.75 \times 20 = \mathbf{215}\)
  • \(Me\): positions 5 and 6 → \((240 + 240)/2 = \mathbf{240}\)
  • \(Q_3\): rank \(0.75 \times 11 = 8.25\) → between 280 (8th) and 300 (9th): \(280 + 0.25 \times 20 = \mathbf{285}\)
  • max = 620
  • \(IQR = 285 - 215 = 70\); fences: \(215 - 1.5 \times 70 = \mathbf{110}\) and \(285 + 1.5 \times 70 = \mathbf{390}\)
  • Outlier: 620 (above the upper fence 390). No low outlier.

Boxplot: whisker from 180 down to 300 (largest value inside the fence), box from 215 to 285 with the median line at 240, and one dot at 620.

Revise: Module 5.

CautionB(d) — centre, raw and grouped (Module 4)
  • Mean: \(\bar{x} = 2770/10 = \mathbf{277}\) €
  • Median: \(\mathbf{240}\) €
  • Mode (raw): \(\mathbf{240}\) € (appears twice)
  • Grouped mean (midpoints 175, 225, 275, 500): \((1\times175 + 5\times225 + 2\times275 + 2\times500)/10 = 2850/10 = \mathbf{285}\) €
  • Grouped mode: modal class \([200,250[\), \(L = 200\), \(h = 50\), \(f_1 = 5\), \(f_0 = 1\), \(f_2 = 2\): \[Mo = 200 + 50 \times \frac{5-1}{(5-1)+(5-2)} = 200 + \frac{200}{7} \approx \mathbf{228.57}\ \text{€}\]

Note the grouped mean (285) differs from the raw mean (277): grouping is an approximation, and the wide last class contributes a lot.

Revise: Modules 3, 4.

CautionB(e) — dispersion (Module 6)

\(\sum x = 2770\), \(\sum x^2 = 909{,}300\). \[\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 = 909{,}300 - \frac{2770^2}{10} = 142{,}010\] \[s^2 = \frac{142{,}010}{9} = \mathbf{15{,}778.89}\ \text{€}^2, \qquad s = \sqrt{15{,}778.89} = \mathbf{125.61}\ \text{€}, \qquad CV = \frac{125.61}{277} \times 100\% \approx \mathbf{45.3\%}\] High relative variability (CV well above 35%).

Revise: Module 6.

CautionB(f) — Fisher–Pearson skewness by hand (Module 7)

Work in tens of €: deviations \(d_i = (x_i - 277)/10\): \(-9.7,\ -7.7,\ -5.7,\ -4.7,\ -3.7,\ -3.7,\ -1.7,\ 0.3,\ 2.3,\ 34.3\) Cubes: \(-912.67,\ -456.53,\ -185.19,\ -103.82,\ -50.65,\ -50.65,\ -4.91,\ 0.03,\ 12.17,\ 40{,}353.61\); sum \(\approx 38{,}601.36\). With \(s/10 = 12.5614\): \[\text{SKEW} = \frac{10}{9 \times 8} \times \frac{38{,}601.36}{12.5614^3} \approx 0.1389 \times \frac{38{,}601.36}{1{,}982.05} \approx \mathbf{+2.70}\] Strong right skew — consistent with the ordering mean (277) > median (240) = mode (240) and with the €620 outlier dragging the right tail.

Revise: Module 7.

6.3 Part C

CautionC(a) — types and frequency tables (Modules 1–2)
  • weather: qualitative nominal; promo_active: qualitative nominal (binary); satisfaction_rating: qualitative ordinal (order 1 < 2 < … < 5, but gaps not equal → median, not mean).
weather \(n_i\) \(f_i\)
Sunny 4 40%
Cloudy 4 40%
Rainy 2 20%

Modal category: Sunny and Cloudy (tie, 4 each).

satisfaction 1 2 3 4 5
\(n_i\) 1 2 3 3 1

Modal ratings: 3 and 4 (tie). Median: sorted 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5 → \((3+3)/2 = \mathbf{3}\).

Revise: Modules 1, 2.

CautionC(b) — five-number summary of revenue (Module 5)

Sorted revenue: 119.97, 137.45, 166.48, 267.04, 271.02, 297.20, 329.19, 401.30, 549.51, 634.78.

  • min = 119.97
  • \(Q_1\): rank 2.75 → \(137.45 + 0.75 \times (166.48 - 137.45) = \mathbf{159.22}\)
  • \(Me = (271.02 + 297.20)/2 = \mathbf{284.11}\)
  • \(Q_3\): rank 8.25 → \(401.30 + 0.25 \times (549.51 - 401.30) = \mathbf{438.35}\)
  • max = 634.78
  • \(IQR = 438.35 - 159.22 = 279.13\); fences: \(159.22 - 418.70 = \mathbf{-259.47}\) and \(438.35 + 418.70 = \mathbf{857.05}\)
  • No outliers: both extremes lie inside the fences.

Boxplot: box from 159.22 to 438.35, median line at 284.11, whiskers out to 119.97 and 634.78.

Revise: Module 5.

CautionC(c) — centre and spread of revenue (Modules 4, 6)

\(\sum y = 3173.94\), so \(\bar{y} = \mathbf{317.39}\) €. \[s^2 = \frac{1}{9}\sum(y_i - \bar{y})^2 = \mathbf{29{,}001.78}\ \text{€}^2, \quad s = \mathbf{170.30}\ \text{€}, \quad CV = \frac{170.30}{317.39} \times 100\% \approx \mathbf{53.7\%}\] Mean (317.39) > median (284.11): the gap suggests a mild right skew (indeed SKEW ≈ +0.78 on these 10 days) — and very high relative variability (CV ≈ 54%).

Revise: Modules 4, 6, 7.

CautionC(d) — contingency table promo × revenue class (Module 8)

Counts:

promo_active \([0,200[\) \([200,400[\) \([400,600[\) \([600,900[\) total
No 2 4 0 1 7
Yes 1 0 2 0 3

Row percentages (conditional distribution of revenue given promo status):

promo_active \([0,200[\) \([200,400[\) \([400,600[\) \([600,900[\)
No 28.6% 57.1% 0% 14.3%
Yes 33.3% 0% 66.7% 0%

The row profiles differ sharply: 2 of 3 promo days (66.7%) earn €400–600, against 1 of 7 non-promo days (14.3%) — and no promo day falls in \([200,400[\). Visible dependence (keep the tiny sample in mind: association, not proof of causation).

Revise: Module 8.

CautionC(e) — customer_count vs revenue (Module 9)

  1. The scatter plot is a strong, upward, roughly linear cloud: more customers, more revenue.

  2. Computational table (\(n = 10\)): \[\sum x = 233,\quad \sum y = 3173.94,\quad \sum x^2 = 6607,\quad \sum y^2 = 1{,}268{,}405.57,\quad \sum xy = 91{,}109.47\] \[\bar{x} = 23.3,\quad \bar{y} = 317.394,\quad s_X = 11.44,\quad s_Y = 170.30\] \[\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \frac{1}{9}\sum(x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y}) = \mathbf{1906.30}\ \text{cust·€}\] \[r = \frac{1906.30}{11.44 \times 170.30} = \mathbf{0.978}, \qquad r^2 = \mathbf{0.957}\] \[b = \frac{\text{Cov}(X,Y)}{s_X^2} = \frac{1906.30}{11.44^2} = \mathbf{14.56}\ \text{€ per customer}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x} = 317.394 - 14.563 \times 23.3 = \mathbf{-21.92}\ \text{€}\]

  3. Prediction at 40 customers: \(\hat{y} = -21.92 + 14.56 \times 40 = \mathbf{560.60}\) €. Reliable: 40 lies inside the observed range (7–46 customers) and \(r^2 = 0.957\).

  4. Check: \(a + b\,\bar{x} = -21.92 + 14.563 \times 23.3 = 317.39 = \bar{y}\) ✓ — the line passes through the point of means.

Revise: Module 9.

6.4 Part D

CautionD1 — payroll-skew (Modules 4, 5, 7)

Sum of the 11 salaries = 519,537 €, so mean = 47,230.64 €. Sorted, the 6th value is 38,050 €, so median = 38,050 €. Report the median: the CEO’s €180,000 is a \(1.5 \times IQR\) outlier (fences ≈ [−2,914; 72,878]) and the distribution is strongly right-skewed (SKEW ≈ +3.12), so the mean is pulled far above what a typical employee earns.

Revise: Modules 4, 5, 7.

CautionD2 — Campus correlation and extrapolation (Module 9)
  1. \(r = 0.970\): a very strong positive linear association between customer count and revenue. \(r^2 = 0.940\): 94% of the variability of revenue is explained by customer count.
  2. \(\hat{y} = -22.75 + 12.30 \times 120 = \mathbf{1453.25}\) €. Not reliable: the observed customer counts run from 3 to 106, so 120 customers is extrapolation beyond the data range.

Revise: Module 9.

CautionD3 — do promotions help? (Modules 8, 10)

Yes — on these 10 days the association is visible: two of the three promo days earned €400–600, against only one of seven non-promo days. But three promo days is a very small sample, and a contingency table shows association, not causation (promos may simply be scheduled on already-busy days). Recommend more promo days before concluding.

Revise: Modules 8, 10.
