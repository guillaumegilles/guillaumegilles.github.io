Module 8 — Bivariate I: contingency tables and scatter plots

Data Description | Part III — Two variables and synthesis

Modified

October 17, 2026

The business question. Every univariate indicator so far described one variable at a time. The real question behind “The Wandering Fork”’s trading is whether two variables move together — does warmer weather really bring more revenue? Do promotions work better on sunny days? This module introduces the two graphical tools for answering that: the contingency table (for two qualitative-or-grouped variables) and the scatter plot (for two quantitative variables). We start with a table and five days small enough to handle by hand, and only then write the notation.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Build a contingency table by hand from a list of individuals, and read a cell count \(n_{ij}\) and the margins \(n_{i\cdot}\), \(n_{\cdot j}\) aloud.
  • Compute row and column marginal distributions from a contingency table, as \(n_{i\cdot}/n\) and \(n_{\cdot j}/n\).
  • Compute a conditional distribution — the distribution of one variable restricted to a single modality of the other — and use it to compare sub-groups fairly.
  • Read a share such as \(n_{ij}/n_{i\cdot}\) aloud as a sentence, and write a share described in words in this notation.
  • Draw a scatter plot of two quantitative variables by hand and describe the pattern it shows (direction, form, exceptions).
  • Distinguish “a visible pattern” from “a proven relationship” — formalised numerically in Module 9.

Theory

Sun, rain and medium days

One Wandering Fork truck classified 50 of its trading days by weather (keeping only Sunny and Rainy days) and by revenue level: Low (under €500), Medium (€500 to under €700) and High (€700 or more). The numbers are a simplified extract chosen so that you can compute everything by hand.

Weather  Revenue Low Medium High
Sunny 3 9 18
Rainy 12 6 2

The truck had 9 medium days when it was sunny, and only 6 when it rained. Is a medium day more likely when the sun is out? And what would you tell the owner about High days: is “18 against 2” the right way to compare sunny and rainy weather?

In plain words: a contingency table is a two-dimensional frequency table. The margins are the row and column totals — they give you back the one-variable frequency tables you already know.

NoteDefinition — contingency table

A contingency table (or cross-tabulation) crosses the modalities of two variables observed on the same individuals: cell \((i,j)\) holds the count \(n_{ij}\) of individuals with modality \(i\) of the first variable and modality \(j\) of the second. The row margin \(n_{i\cdot} = \sum_j n_{ij}\) and column margin \(n_{\cdot j} = \sum_i n_{ij}\) recover each variable’s own (univariate) frequency table from the same data.

NoteNotation decoder — contingency table
Symbol Read aloud In our example (ten days)
\((i,j)\) “cell i, j” — row first, then column \((1,3)\) is the Sunny–High cell
\(n_{ij}\) “n sub i j” \(n_{13} = 3\) Sunny and High days
\(n_{i\cdot}\) “n sub i dot” — the dot replaces the index that was added up \(n_{1\cdot} = 6\) Sunny days
\(n_{\cdot j}\) “n sub dot j” \(n_{\cdot 3} = 4\) High days
\(\sum_j\) “add up over all columns j” across a row: \(1+2+3 = 6\)
\(\sum_i\) “add up over all rows i” down a column: \(3+1 = 4\)

Whole formula as a sentence: “the row margin \(n_{i\cdot}\) is the sum of the counts across row \(i\); the column margin \(n_{\cdot j}\) is the sum of the counts down column \(j\).” Adding all the cells gives the grand total \(n = 10\).

The truck had 30 Sunny days (\(30/50 = 60\%\)) and 20 Rainy days (\(40\%\)). Separately, 40% of all days were High. Notice that Sunny days are simply more numerous — this already explains part of “9 against 6”.

See it. The two margins are two ordinary one-variable bar charts (each block = 10% of the 50 days):

Variable Modality Share of all 50 days
Weather (row margin) Sunny ██████ 60%
Rainy ████ 40%
Revenue (column margin) Low ███ 30%
Medium ███ 30%
High ████ 40%

Recall. In Module 2 you computed relative frequencies \(f_i = n_i/n\) for one variable. A marginal distribution is exactly that, using the margins of the table as the counts.

In plain words: a marginal distribution is the share of all \(n\) individuals in each modality of one variable, read off the edge of the table — as if the other variable did not exist.

TipFormula — marginal distributions

Dividing each margin by the grand total \(n\) gives the two marginal distributions (relative frequencies, as in Module 2): \[ \frac{n_{i\cdot}}{n} \quad \text{(row variable)}, \qquad \frac{n_{\cdot j}}{n} \quad \text{(column variable)}. \] Each one sums to 1 (100%) and ignores the other variable entirely.

NoteNotation decoder — marginal distributions
Symbol Read aloud In our example (50 days)
\(n\) “n” 50 days
\(n_{i\cdot}\) “n sub i dot” row totals: \(n_{1\cdot} = 30\), \(n_{2\cdot} = 20\)
\(n_{\cdot j}\) “n sub dot j” column totals: 15, 15, 20
\(\frac{n_{i\cdot}}{n}\) “n sub i dot over n” \(30/50 = 60\%\) Sunny, \(20/50 = 40\%\) Rainy
\(\frac{n_{\cdot j}}{n}\) “n sub dot j over n” \(15/50 = 30\%\), 30%, \(20/50 = 40\%\)

Whole formula as a sentence: “the marginal share of a modality is its row (or column) total divided by the grand total.”

Conditional distribution

Try it. To answer the hook, compute percentages within each row: divide every cell by its own row total (30 for Sunny, 20 for Rainy), not by 50.

Weather  Revenue Low Medium High Total
Sunny (\(\div 30\)) ? ? ? 100%
Rainy (\(\div 20\)) ? ? ? 100%
All days (\(\div 50\)) 30% 30% 40% 100%

  2. How do High days compare between the two kinds of weather?
  3. Now read down the High column instead: of the 20 High days, what share were Sunny?
CautionTry it — check your answer
Weather  Revenue Low Medium High Total
Sunny (\(\div 30\)) 10% 30% 60% 100%
Rainy (\(\div 20\)) 60% 30% 10% 100%
All days (\(\div 50\)) 30% 30% 40% 100%
  1. No: \(9/30 = 30\%\) and \(6/20 = 30\%\). “9 against 6” only reflects that there were more Sunny days.
  2. \(18/30 = 60\%\) of Sunny days were High, against \(2/20 = 10\%\) of Rainy days: here the weather really makes a difference.
  3. \(18/20 = 90\%\) of High days were Sunny. This is a different question (“given High”) from question 2 (“given Sunny”), so it has a different answer.

See it. Each row now has its own 100% bar (each block = 10% of that row):

Given Low Medium High
Sunny █ 10% ███ 30% ██████ 60%
Rainy ██████ 60% ███ 30% █ 10%
All days (marginal) ███ 30% ███ 30% ████ 40%

The Medium column is identical in all three rows; the Low and High columns are not — that is where weather and revenue are associated.

In plain words: to compare Sunny vs Rainy days fairly, don’t compare raw counts (Sunny has more days total). Instead, compute percentages within each weather type — that’s the conditional distribution.

NoteDefinition — conditional distribution

A conditional distribution is the distribution of one variable restricted to a single modality of the other — one row (or one column), rescaled to its own row (or column) total instead of the grand total \(n\). It answers “given this category, how is the other variable distributed?”, which the raw counts alone cannot, since row totals differ in size.

TipFormula — conditional distributions

Given row modality \(i\), the share of column modality \(j\) is \[ \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}} \quad \text{(row percentages)}, \] and given column modality \(j\), the share of row modality \(i\) is \[ \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{\cdot j}} \quad \text{(column percentages)}. \] Each conditional distribution sums to 1 (100%) over its own row (or column).

NoteNotation decoder — conditional distributions
Symbol Read aloud In our example (50 days)
\(n_{ij}\) “n sub i j” \(n_{13} = 18\) Sunny and High days
\(n_{i\cdot}\) “n sub i dot” \(n_{1\cdot} = 30\) Sunny days
\(n_{\cdot j}\) “n sub dot j” \(n_{\cdot 3} = 20\) High days
\(\frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}}\) “n sub i j over n sub i dot” — “given row i” \(18/30 = 60\%\) of Sunny days were High
\(\frac{n_{ij}}{n_{\cdot j}}\) “n sub i j over n sub dot j” — “given column j” \(18/20 = 90\%\) of High days were Sunny

Whole formula as a sentence: “the conditional share is the count in one cell divided by the total of the row (or column) you are restricting to — not by the grand total.”

See it. Step 1 of the figure shows the five points; step 2 adds the two mean lines and colours each point by the quarter it falls in.

temps = [12, 16, 21, 24, 27]
items = [40, 55, 50, 70, 85]

avg = xs => xs.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0) / xs.length

function quadrants(xs, ys) {
  const mx = avg(xs), my = avg(ys);
  const agree = [], against = [];
  xs.forEach((v, i) =>
    ((v - mx) * (ys[i] - my) > 0 ? agree : against).push(i + 1));
  return {mx, my, agree, against};
}

In plain words: each day becomes a dot at (temperature, revenue). The cloud of dots shows the pattern — upward trend, downward trend, curved, or no pattern at all.

NoteDefinition — scatter plot

A scatter plot places one point per individual, using one quantitative variable for the \(x\)-coordinate and another for the \(y\)-coordinate. It is the standard first look at a possible relationship between two quantitative variables, before computing any numerical summary.

Worked example

Contingency table — weather × daily_revenue_eur class

From “The Wandering Fork”’s Harbour 30-day subset (wandering-fork.xlsx, sheet harbour_30d):

Weather  Revenue class [400,500[ [500,600[ [600,700[ [700,800[ [800,900[ Row total
Sunny 0 3 6 3 1 13
Cloudy 3 2 2 3 2 12
Rainy 3 1 0 1 0 5
Column total 6 6 8 7 3 30

Step 1 — check the margins. The row totals (13, 12, 5) match Module 2’s weather frequency table exactly, and the column totals (6, 6, 8, 7, 3) match Module 3’s daily_revenue_eur class table exactly — both are recovered as marginal distributions of the same 30 rows, confirming the table was built correctly.

Step 2 — read the pattern. All 6 days in the lowest revenue class [400,500[ were either Cloudy or Rainy — zero Sunny days earned under €500. Conversely, Sunny days account for 10 of the 18 days earning €600 or more, including 6 of the 8 days in [600,700[. This is a first, purely visual hint that better weather is associated with higher revenue.

Step 3 — conditional distribution. Restricting to Sunny days only (row total 13) and rescaling each cell by 13 instead of 30 gives the conditional distribution of revenue class given Sunny: \(0\%, 23.1\%, 46.2\%, 23.1\%, 7.7\%\) for the five classes (rounded; they sum to \(100\%\) before rounding) — compare this to the unconditional column percentages from Module 3 (20.0%, 20.0%, 26.7%, 23.3%, 10.0%): Sunny days are visibly shifted toward the higher revenue classes, confirming the pattern from Step 2 with actual percentages instead of raw counts.

Scatter plot — temperature_c vs daily_revenue_eur

Plotting temperature_c (x-axis) against daily_revenue_eur (y-axis) for all 30 Harbour days shows points trending upward and to the right: as temperature rises, revenue tends to rise too, though the points do not fall on a perfect line — there is visible scatter around the trend.

Proof / derivation

CautionDerivation — marginal totals recover the univariate frequency table (click to expand)

Let \(n_{ij}\) be the contingency table’s cell counts, with row modalities \(i=1,\dots,r\) and column modalities \(j=1,\dots,c\). Every one of the \(n\) individuals is counted in exactly one cell, so summing across all columns for a fixed row \(i\) recovers that row modality’s total univariate count: \[ n_{i\cdot} = \sum_{j=1}^{c} n_{ij}. \] For “The Wandering Fork”, row \(i=\text{Sunny}\): \(0+3+6+3+1 = 13\), matching Module 2’s Sunny count exactly — this is not a coincidence but a direct consequence of the contingency table partitioning the same 30 rows both by weather and, independently, by daily_revenue_eur class.

Visual intuition

A contingency table is a two-dimensional histogram: instead of one row of class counts (Module 3), we now have a grid of counts, and reading down a single column or across a single row projects the two-dimensional picture back onto one axis — exactly recovering the one-variable-at-a-time view from Modules 2–3. A scatter plot’s “upward-and-to-the-right” shape is the visual signature of a positive relationship; Module 9 replaces “shape” with a single number, the correlation coefficient.

Step 2 of the five-day scatter figure shows where that number comes from: the two mean lines cut the plot into four quarters, and a rising cloud puts most points in the lower-left and upper-right quarters (4 of 5 here). Module 9’s covariance adds up exactly this.

(c) \(\frac{n_{31}}{n_{\cdot 1}} = 3/6 = 50\%\): “half of the days under €500 were Rainy”. The cell is the same, but (b) restricts to the Rainy row and (c) to the [400,500[ column.

Common mistakes

WarningWatch out
  • Using raw counts to compare rows with different totals. Always use conditional distributions (% of row/column total) for fair comparison.
  • Confusing marginal and conditional distributions. Marginal = overall (ignoring the other variable); conditional = given a specific value of the other variable.
  • Reading a scatter plot as proof of causation. A pattern only shows association; Module 9 quantifies the linear association, but neither proves causation.
  • Forgetting to group continuous variables for contingency tables. daily_revenue_eur must be binned (e.g., €100 classes) before crossing with weather — PivotTable grouping does this automatically.
  • Using a line chart instead of a scatter plot for two quantitative variables. Line charts imply a sequence (time); scatter plots show the relationship between two variables measured on the same individuals.

Exit check

Five minutes, no calculator needed except for question 4.

  1. In one sentence, what is the difference between a marginal and a conditional distribution?
  2. Why compare row percentages rather than raw counts when the row totals differ?
  3. Read \(n_{\cdot j} = \sum_i n_{ij}\) aloud as a sentence.
  4. Harbour had 12 good days and 8 bad days; Campus had 9 good days and 21 bad days. Compute the share of good days given each franchise.
  5. A scatter plot of temperature and items sold rises to the right. Can you conclude that heat causes the extra sales? Why?
CautionExit check — answers
  1. A marginal distribution describes one variable over all \(n\) individuals, ignoring the other; a conditional distribution describes it within one modality of the other variable only.
  2. Because a bigger row has bigger counts even when nothing differs: in the hook, 9 vs 6 medium days were 30% vs 30%.
  3. “The column margin \(n_{\cdot j}\) is the sum, over all rows \(i\), of the counts in column \(j\).”
  4. Harbour: \(12/20 = 60\%\); Campus: \(9/30 = 30\%\) (row totals 20 and 30, not the grand total 50).
  5. No: a scatter plot only shows association. Other factors (season, events, foot traffic) can drive both; Module 9 measures the linear association, but neither proves causation.
